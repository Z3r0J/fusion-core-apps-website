**Best before is about quality and use by is about safety.** Food past its best before date is usually still safe to eat if it was stored properly, but food past its use by date should not be eaten, even if it looks and smells fine, unless you froze or cooked it on or before the date.

That is the rule in the UK and the EU, where both phrases are defined in law. In the US, most dates are voluntary and federal guidance treats even "use by" as a quality date, with one exception: infant formula. "Best by" and "best if used by" are simply the American way of saying best before.

Food date labels confuse almost everyone. "Best before", "use by", "sell by", "best if used by", "display until" — they sound interchangeable, but they mean very different things. Getting them mixed up leads to two costly mistakes: throwing away food that was perfectly fine, or eating something that was genuinely unsafe.

Here is the clear version.

## Best Before vs. Use By: The One Distinction That Matters

There are really only two ideas hiding behind all those labels:

- **Best before = quality.** After this date, food may be past its peak — a little stale, less crisp, slightly faded in flavor — but it is generally still safe to eat.
- **Use by = safety.** This is the date after which perishable, higher-risk food should not be eaten, even if it looks and smells fine.

If you remember nothing else, remember that: **best before is about how good it is; use by is about whether it's safe.**

|  | Best before / best by | Use by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it tells you | When quality may start to drop | When the food is no longer safe to eat (UK and EU) |
| Typical foods | Frozen peas and ice cream, dried pasta and rice, tinned food, cheese, biscuits, crisps | Meat products, ready-to-eat salads, soft cheese, ready meals, smoked fish |
| After the date | Usually safe; check it with sight, smell and taste | Don't eat it, unless you froze or cooked it in time |
| Can you trust your senses? | Yes, for quality | No: unsafe food can look and smell normal |

## Best Before (and Best By), Explained

You will find **best before** dates on shelf-stable and lower-risk foods: pasta, rice, canned goods, crackers, cookies, frozen food, oils, and many condiments. In the US the same idea usually reads **"best by"** or **"best if used by"**. The USDA recommends "Best if Used By" because research shows shoppers understand it as a peak-quality date rather than a deadline.

These dates are the manufacturer's estimate of how long the product stays at its best quality. A can of beans a month past its best before date is not dangerous — it may just be slightly softer or less flavorful. The USDA says high-acid canned foods keep their best quality for 12 to 18 months and low-acid canned foods for two to five years, but any can that is dented, rusted or swollen should be thrown out. Dry pasta can be fine long after the printed date if it has been stored properly.

For these foods, use your judgment. The UK government's guidance suggests three quick checks: look for visible mould on bread, taste biscuits or crisps to see if they are stale, and smell dairy products with a best before date to see if they have soured. The USDA adds that an off odor, flavor or texture means the food has spoiled and should not be eaten. If everything seems right, it usually is.

One exception: eggs. Food Standards Scotland says eggs can be eaten only a day or two after their best before date, and only if cooked thoroughly, because any salmonella present can multiply.

One condition: the best before date only holds if you stored the food the way the label says. The date is guidance, not a hard stop.

## Use By, Explained

**Use by** dates appear on perishable, higher-risk foods: fresh meat and poultry, fish, ready-to-eat meals, soft cheeses, and prepared salads. These are foods where harmful bacteria can grow to dangerous levels without changing how the food looks, smells, or tastes. The UK government puts it plainly: eating food after the use by date could make you ill.

This is the critical part: **you cannot rely on your senses with a use by date.** Food past its use by date can be unsafe even when it seems perfectly normal. Food Standards Scotland goes further and says milk and dairy products with a use by date should never be sniffed or used past it.

### Cook it or freeze it before the date

A use by date really means "eat, cook or freeze by". According to the UK government's guidance:

- **Freeze it on or before the use by date** and it will not deteriorate, because bacteria cannot grow on frozen food. Keep the freezer at -18 °C (0 °F), label the pack with what it is and when you froze it, and use it within 24 hours of defrosting.
- **Cook it on or before the use by date**, then cool it, keep it in the fridge and eat it within 48 hours, or freeze it.
- **Store it as the label says.** If the pack tells you to refrigerate it, keep it at 5 °C or below (US guidance uses 40 °F). Stored badly, food can become unsafe before the use by date.

## Sell By, Display Until and Other Store Dates

- **Sell by** is a stock-rotation date for the store, not for you. It tells the retailer how long to display the item, and the USDA is clear that it is not a safety date. Food is typically still good for some time after a sell by date if stored correctly — but for raw meat and fish that window is short, so go by the fridge times further down.
- **Display until** is the UK version, aimed at shop staff. Back in 2011 the UK government's date-labelling guidance told retailers to remove both "sell by" and "display until" marks to avoid confusing shoppers, though you may still spot them.
- **California went further.** Consumer-facing "sell by" dates are banned there on food made on or after July 1, 2026; retailers can still use coded sell by dates for stock rotation.

Lot codes, can codes and pack dates are also for the supply chain, not for you. More on reading those [below](#how-to-read-a-food-date-label).

## The One Exception in the US: Infant Formula

In the US, infant formula is the only food that federal rules require to carry a date. Under FDA regulations (21 CFR 107.20), the label must show a "Use by" date, chosen by the manufacturer, packer or distributor based on testing.

The date promises two things, as long as the formula is stored and prepared as the label says: it still contains at least the amount of each nutrient listed on the label, and it is still in acceptable condition, for example able to pass through an ordinary bottle nipple.

That makes it a firm limit, not a quality hint. The USDA's advice is simple: do not buy or use infant formula after its use by date.

## US vs. UK and EU Date Labels

The words look the same on both sides of the Atlantic, but the rules behind them are not.

**UK and EU.** Most prepacked foods must show either a use by or a best before date, and the manufacturer picks which one depending on how likely the food is to become unsafe over time. Under Article 24 of Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, highly perishable foods must carry a use by date instead of a best before date, and after it the food "shall be deemed to be unsafe". The UK kept the same rule in its own version of the regulation.

**US.** Except for infant formula, product dating is not required by federal regulations. When a manufacturer does add a date, it must be truthful and not misleading. The USDA and FDA encourage "Best if Used By", and in federal guidance even "use by" means peak quality. In December 2024 the FDA and USDA opened a joint request for information on date labeling (comments closed March 5, 2025), noting an estimate that confusion over date labels accounts for about 20% of food waste in the home. As of October 2026, the USDA's guidance still says dates are not federally required except on infant formula.

**California.** AB 660 standardizes the wording for food made on or after July 1, 2026: quality dates read "BEST if Used by" or "BEST if Used or Frozen by", and safety dates read "USE by" or "USE by or Freeze by". Consumer-facing "sell by" dates are banned, and infant formula, eggs, and beer and other malt beverages are left out.

**What this means for you.** In the UK and EU, a use by date is a legal safety line. In most of the US, it is the manufacturer's quality estimate, but perishables still have short fridge lives. When in doubt on a perishable item, treat the strictest interpretation as the safe one.

## How to Read a Food Date Label

_Illustrative diagram, not a photo of a real product._

### 1. Find the date

Look on the front, the lid, the base of a can or the side of a carton. In the UK and EU, the label can also point you elsewhere ("Best before: see lid"), because the rules allow a reference to where the date is printed. Some marks are simply hard to read: a 2018 European Commission market study judged the legibility of date marks to be poor on 11% of the products it sampled.

### 2. Read the phrase in front of the date

The words decide which rules apply. "Use by" is the safety date in the UK and EU. "Best before", "best by" and "best if used by" are quality dates. "Sell by" and "display until" are for the shop.

### 3. Decode the format

- **UK and EU:** day, month, year, in that order, and never coded. Food that keeps for up to three months can show just the day and month; food that keeps for 3 to 18 months can show the month and year; beyond 18 months, the year alone is enough. That is why a pack of chicken might say "Use by 18 Oct" while dried pasta says "Best before end 03 2027", meaning the end of March 2027.
- **US:** there is no single federal format, because dating is voluntary for everything except infant formula, which shows a month and year. A date like 10/12/26 is October 12 in US order but 10 December in UK and EU order, so check where an imported product was made.

### 4. Ignore codes that are not dates

- **Lot, batch and can codes** are strings of letters and numbers that identify when and where the product was made. The USDA says can codes are for tracking, stock rotation and recalls, and are "not meant for the consumer to interpret" as a best-by date. They are worth checking against recall notices.
- **Egg pack dates** on USDA-graded cartons are a three-digit day of the year, from 001 for January 1 to 365 for December 31. If the carton also shows a sell by date, it can be no more than 30 days after the pack date. In the UK, eggs carry a best before date of no more than 28 days after they are laid.

### 5. Read the storage line next to the date

In the UK and EU, a use by date must be followed by the storage conditions, and a best before date carries them when they matter. The date only holds if you follow them, including any "once opened" instructions. Food that is not stored properly can become unsafe before its use by date or lose quality before its best before date.

### Foods with no date at all

Not every food needs one. In the UK and EU, best before dates are not required on uncut fresh fruit and vegetables (sprouting seeds aside), wine, drinks with 10% or more alcohol, bakery goods normally eaten within 24 hours, vinegar, cooking salt, solid sugar, sugar confectionery and chewing gum. In the US, federal rules do not require a date on any food except infant formula, though some states set their own rules for items like eggs.

## When It's Safe to Eat Past the Date — and When It Isn't

A simple rule of thumb:

- **Shelf-stable food past its best before date:** usually fine. Inspect it, trust your senses, use common sense.
- **Perishable food past its use by date:** do not eat it. This is the line you do not cross.
- **Food you froze or cooked before its use by date:** fine, within the 24-hour (defrosted) and 48-hour (cooked) windows above.
- **Infant formula past its use by date:** never.

Freezing is your best friend here. Freezing food before its use by date effectively pauses the clock, so a use by date is really a "use or freeze by" date.

### US fridge times for perishables

In the US, where most dates describe quality, the more useful number for perishables is how long they keep in the fridge. These are the limits from the federal FoodSafety.gov cold storage chart for a fridge at 40 °F (4 °C) or below. In its words, these short limits "will help keep them from spoiling or becoming dangerous to eat".

| Food | Fridge time (40 °F or below) |
| --- | --- |
| Raw chicken or turkey | 1 to 2 days |
| Raw ground meat | 1 to 2 days |
| Raw steaks, chops and roasts | 3 to 5 days |
| Fatty fish | 1 to 3 days |
| Raw eggs in the shell | 3 to 5 weeks |
| Hot dogs | 2 weeks unopened, 1 week opened |
| Luncheon meat | 2 weeks unopened, 3 to 5 days opened or deli-sliced |

### What spoilage signs do and don't tell you

Spoilage bacteria cause off odors, flavors and textures, and food with those signs should not be eaten. The reverse does not hold for use by foods: no smell does not mean safe. And a change in the color of meat or poultry is not, on its own, a sign of spoilage, according to the USDA. If you cannot tell how a perishable food was stored or what its date means, do not eat it.

## How to Waste Less Food

Food waste is a genuinely large problem. The UN Environment Programme's Food Waste Index Report 2024 estimates that 1.05 billion tonnes of food waste (including inedible parts like peels and bones) were generated in 2022, and that households were responsible for 60% of it — more than shops and restaurants combined. That means everyday habits at home make a real difference.

Date labels are part of the story. A 2018 European Commission study estimates that up to 10% of the food waste generated in the EU each year is linked to date marking, and the FDA has cited an estimate that date-label confusion accounts for about 20% of food waste in the home.

The good news: a few small habits make a real dent.

- **Shop your kitchen first.** Check what you already have before buying more. It is hard to use food you forgot you owned.
- **Plan around what expires soonest.** Cook the items closest to their date first — "eat me first" beats "eat me whenever".
- **Store food properly.** The right temperature keeps both dates valid; poor storage can make food unsafe before its use by date.
- **Freeze before the date.** If you will not get to something in time, freeze it while it is still good.
- **Look past the store dates.** Do not bin food just because its sell by or display until date has passed.
- **Keep track of what you have.** You cannot use what you have forgotten about. This is the single biggest lever, and it is the hardest to do from memory alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between best before and use by?

Best before is about quality: after it, food may lose flavor or texture but is usually still safe to eat if it was stored properly. Use by is about safety: in the UK and EU, don't eat food after its use by date, even if it looks and smells fine, unless you froze or cooked it on or before the date.

### Can I eat food after the best before date?

Usually yes. Best before is a quality guide, not a safety cutoff. Check the food first: look for mould, taste dry foods like biscuits or crisps for staleness, and smell dairy for sourness. Throw away cans that are dented, rusted or swollen, and never apply this logic to a use by date. One exception: eggs. Food Standards Scotland says eggs can be eaten only a day or two after their best before date, and only if cooked thoroughly, because any salmonella present can multiply.

### Can you eat food after the use by date?

In the UK and EU, no. Eating food after its use by date could make you ill, even if it looks and smells fine. The exception is food you froze or cooked on or before the date. In the US, federal guidance treats use by as a quality date except on infant formula, but perishables like raw chicken and ground meat still keep only 1 to 2 days in the fridge.

### Is best by the same as best before?

Yes. Both are quality dates. Best by and best if used by are the usual US wording, and Best if Used By is the phrase the USDA and FDA recommend. Best before is the UK and EU wording, shown as best before end when the date has only a month or a year.

### Is use by the same as expiration date?

Not exactly. UK and EU food law uses use by for safety and doesn't define an expiration date. In the US, dates are voluntary except on infant formula, and an EXP date on an egg carton may be required by state law; it isn't a federal requirement. On perishable food, the cautious move is to treat any expiration date like a use by date.

### What does sell by mean?

A sell by date tells the store how long to display a product for sale. It is for stock control, not safety, so you can still eat the food after it if you store it properly and use perishables within normal fridge times. California bans consumer-facing sell by dates on food made on or after July 1, 2026.

### Can I freeze food on its use by date?

Yes, as long as you freeze it on or before the use by date, never after. Bacteria can't grow on frozen food, so it won't deteriorate in the freezer. Keep the freezer at -18 °C (0 °F), follow the pack's freezing and defrosting instructions, and eat the food within 24 hours of defrosting.

### Which foods don't need a date label?

In the UK and EU, best before dates aren't required on uncut fresh fruit and vegetables, wine, drinks with 10% or more alcohol, bakery goods normally eaten within 24 hours, vinegar, cooking salt, solid sugar, sugar confectionery and chewing gum. In the US, only infant formula is federally required to carry a date.

### How can I stop wasting food?

Plan meals around what expires soonest, store food at the right temperature, freeze items before their date, and keep track of what you have. A tracker like Expiroo, now in closed beta, reminds you before food goes bad so you can use it in time.
